# Style patterns

Use these as demonstrations of editorial moves, not stock phrases to paste into
every script. Treat all example measurements below as fictional supplied inputs,
not facts about a real product.

## Open on something the viewer can judge

Weak: "Welcome back! Today I'm excited to show you our amazing new remote desktop."
Better: *[Move a window with the browser frame visible.]* "That desktop is on another
machine. I'm controlling it from this tab."
Move: put the action before the announcement, then identify the product.

Weak: "Our new developer tool significantly accelerates the test workflow."
Better, given a supplied four-second run: *[Run the tests.]* "Four seconds. That's
the whole test suite. Let me show you what changed."
Move: make the result specific and filmable; show the actual run rather than imply
that an edited time jump proves speed.

Weak: "Security and collaboration are at the heart of our platform."
Better, given an actual sharing control: *[Open the access panel.]* "Before I send
this, here's exactly who can open it."
Move: show a meaningful user decision rather than recite values.

Vary the opening device: a visible result, a real task completed, an unexpected
setup, or a comparison the viewer can see. Do not begin every remote-control script
with "Looks local, doesn't it?"

## Translate the mechanism, not just the vocabulary

Given: a cache reuses unchanged results instead of rebuilding them.
Weak: "An optimized caching architecture unlocks next-generation performance."
Better: "The old build repeated work that hadn't changed. This one reuses those
results and rebuilds the part I actually edited."
Move: name the waste, the change, and the practical consequence.

Given: one benchmark shows a change from 12 seconds to 4 seconds, with the same
suite and machine explicitly supplied.
Weak: "Three times faster, guaranteed."
Better: "On this machine, the same suite went from twelve seconds to four."
Move: retain the denominator and scope; leave the numbers to persuade.

Given: the presenter reports that typing feels responsive, without latency data.
Weak: "Instant input. Absolutely no lag."
Better: "Type a line. Make a correction. That feels local."
Move: use a first-person impression and give the viewer a chance to judge it.

## Put humor inside the demonstration

Good: *[Correct a typo.]* "The connection is keeping up. My spelling is not."
Good: after a verified reduction in repeated work, "It finally stopped doing the
same job twice. I'm trying to learn from it."
Good: after a genuinely brief wait, "That was most of my excuse to make coffee."

Make the joke depend on what just happened. Use self-deprecation sparingly; let the
presenter remain competent. Allow a restrained callback near the end only when it
adds something, not because every script needs a comedy arc.

Avoid: jokes at the viewer's expense, jokes about stealing screens or credentials,
random pop-culture quotes, "insert laugh here," or a punchline after every feature.
Avoid stacking all three good examples into one script.

## Keep the contrast without turning it into a slogan

Weak: "Not just fast. Not just secure. Not just simple. Revolutionary."
Better: "The frame counter is useful. Now let's actually use the desktop."
Move: make a transition that motivates the next shot rather than another adjective.

Weak: "Performance without compromise. Freedom without limits."
Better: "Here is the setting I changed, and here is the result on this machine."
Move: replace an absolute claim with an inspectable explanation.

Weak: "It just works."
Better: show the ordinary task working and describe only the relevant conditions.
Move: let the footage carry confidence.

## Close with somewhere to go

Use an invitation suited to the audience: "Try it on your own setup. The project
link is below," or "The implementation is in the repository. Take a look."
Mention open-source status or self-hosting only when supported for that product.
Use "links below" only for a publishing format where those links can be supplied.
Do not inherit "Talk to you soon" from the Roomler example unless it suits the
presenter. End the narration cleanly; do not append an offer to rewrite it again.
