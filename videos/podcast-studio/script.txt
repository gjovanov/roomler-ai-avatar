# [Title, then both cameras.]
helen: Welcome to the show. My guest today built a remote desktop because his wife swore at TeamViewer. Goran, welcome.
goran3f: Thanks for having me. And for the record, she still swears. Just not at remote desktops anymore.

# [Guest cam beside the "What is Roomler?" cards.]
goran3f: One open-source app that replaces three. Remote desktop from any browser, like TeamViewer. A private network between your machines, like Tailscale. And chat and video calls for the team, like Teams.
helen: Three products. One person. Did you sleep at all?
goran3f: My wife says no. She also says she should get more credit for writing the product specification. Which is a fair point.
helen: Hahaha. I think I'm on her side. Okay, take me back to that night. What actually happened?
goran3f: Friday night. I'm coding, she's next to me, doing accounting on an office computer through TeamViewer. The only machine with the license.
goran3f: She closes the session by accident. Nobody at the office to send her a new password. The work waits until Monday.
helen: Oh no.
goran3f: Oh yes. And then I heard what I can only describe as hot, spicy, juicy swearing. Followed by one calm sentence: there must be a better way...
helen: And you thought, challenge accepted.
goran3f: Well, I had built video conferencing before. A remote desktop is the same machinery pointed one way. How hard could it be?
helen: Yeah, with those words you underestimated your enemy, didn't you?
goran3f: Indeed, I did. However, seven months later, she and a few colleagues were the first users. So technically, my first customer is also my toughest critic.
helen: Ha! Of course she is. Anyway, shifting gears a bit. Let's talk speed, because your videos keep showing frame counters. A MacBook at a hundred and twenty frames a second. Why does anyone need that?
goran3f: Nobody needs it. Everybody wants it. It's a sports car for your mouse pointer.
helen: Ha! I like that.
# [The MacBook footage at 120 fps with its counter (macbook-120fps).]
goran3f: On the Mac it's almost unfair. Apple's media engine encodes a whole frame in about six milliseconds. At a hundred and twenty frames a second, you only get eight.
helen: Oh, I get it. Six milliseconds of work in an eight millisecond slot. And Windows? I assume Windows made it difficult.
# [The windows-120fps terminal: 59 versus 195 fps.]
goran3f: Windows had a hidden speed limit. It hands a screen-capture app one frame every sixteen milliseconds, unless the app asks for more.
helen: So the fix was simply to ask?
goran3f: Yeah, that's right. Like a waiter who won't bring the menu until you wave. So now Roomler waves.
helen: Hahaha! That's brilliant.
# [The 0.4.174 trial: the clock pin, 112.4 fps, Proven.]
goran3f: Then the graphics card tried to save power in the middle of a session, so Roomler now keeps it awake while it streams fast. A hundred and twelve frames a second, proven in the log.
helen: Nice. On the flip side, I saw fifty-six frames on a Mac mini running Linux. What happened there?
goran3f: That one is my favorite, because it's the hardest. Asahi Linux has no video encoder driver for that chip yet, so every frame is encoded by the CPU.
# [The wayland-asahi footage: the KDE desktop, the counter at 56.]
goran3f: Roomler just tells the processor: while I'm encoding, give me the fast core. Fifty-six frames a second, with no hardware help at all.
helen: So the CPU does the heavy lifting and you take the credit.
goran3f: Some people call it management.
helen: Hahaha, nicely reframed. Speaking of things that quietly do the heavy lifting, you said private network. Show me the Tailscale part.
# [The mesh-network footage: the peers table, names and addresses boxed.]
goran3f: Every machine I own, in one list. The MacBook is direct, a few milliseconds away. The servers are direct across the internet.
goran3f: And when there's no direct path, it goes through a relay on port four four three, the same door every website uses. Slower, but it connects.
helen: Help me understand this part. Can the relay read my traffic?
# [The relay diagram.]
goran3f: No. It forwards encrypted WireGuard packets it can't open. Think of a postman who can't read the letters.
helen: Ha! A very frustrated postman.
# [SSH into the Mac: no sshd, no open port.]
goran3f: On top of that, over the same network you get SSH and remote commands, with no SSH server and no open port. Off by default, and the server can't switch them on by itself. The machine has to say yes first.
helen: Yeah, that makes sense. Let's step back for a moment. Who is this actually for?
# [Guest cam beside the use-case cards.]
goran3f: Anyone who has ever driven across town to fix a computer. Families helping families. Agencies and contractors working inside client infrastructure.
goran3f: People who want their home machine from the office, and it should feel local. And companies that want to host the whole thing themselves, with no license key and no device limit.
helen: Okay, I'm convinced. Moving on to the practical part. How do I install it? Please tell me it's not a weekend project.
# [The real install page, highlighted in turn.]
goran3f: Two ways. On Windows there's a setup wizard: pick a role, click next, done. For people who like clicking.
goran3f: And for people who like typing, one line in a terminal, on Windows, Linux or macOS. You paste a one-time enrollment code from your account, and the machine shows up in your devices.
helen: And which kind are you?
goran3f: I wrote both, so whichever one breaks, I'm the one to blame.
helen: Hahaha. Fair enough. Last question. Anything you want to say to your wife?
